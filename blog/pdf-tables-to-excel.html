How to convert PDF tables to Excel

A PDF doesn't know what a row or a column is. That's why copying a table out of one usually destroys the layout. Here are three ways to get the data into a spreadsheet — including one that never uploads your file.

Why PDF tables are so hard to reuse

A PDF was designed to preserve a printed page, not a data grid. It knows where each character sits on the page, but it has no concept of "row 14, column C." That's why copying a table out of a PDF often produces a single column of numbers with the alignment destroyed.

A good converter has to rebuild the grid from scratch: detect ruling lines, cluster text into cells, and then decide which blocks belong together. The better the source, the cleaner the result.

Method 1: Browser-based converter (free, nothing uploaded)

Open the PDF to Excel converter, drop your file, and click Convert. It reads the text blocks, snaps them to a row/column grid, and hands you an .xlsx and a .csv — you pick whichever your spreadsheet app likes.

Best for: Bank statements, invoices, price lists, survey exports, and any table you'd otherwise retype by hand.

Because it runs in your browser, financial documents never touch a third-party server. That matters more than people think — a bank statement contains your address, account number, and spending history.

Method 2: Adobe Acrobat Pro (paid, cloud)

Acrobat Pro → Export PDF → Spreadsheet → Microsoft Excel Workbook. It's the most accurate option for messy layouts, and it lets you hand-correct the table borders before export. Downsides: $22.99/month, and files are processed in Adobe's cloud unless you disable it in preferences.

Method 3: Google Drive / Sheets (free, but uploads)

Upload the PDF to Drive, right-click → Open with → Google Sheets. Google creates a new spreadsheet with the extracted text. It's free and fast, but multi-page tables usually land in the wrong cells, and the file is stored on Google's servers.

Fine for a one-off price list. Not ideal for anything confidential.

Common problems and fixes

"Merged cells collapse into one"

Merged header cells are the hardest case. If your spreadsheet comes out with headers shoved into one column, export the .csv instead and split it in your spreadsheet app using a fixed delimiter. Slower, but predictable.

"Numbers arrive as text"

Common with currencies and thousands separators. In Excel, select the column → Data → Text to Columns → Finish. In Google Sheets, use =VALUE(SUBSTITUTE(A1,",","")). Also watch for European decimal commas — swap , for . first.

"A table spanning two pages got split wrongly"

Extract page by page. Use Split PDF to cut the document at the table boundary, convert each part, then stack the results. Far more reliable than hoping the converter guesses right.

"Nothing comes out at all"

Then the PDF is probably a scan — a picture of a table, not real text. Run it through PDF OCR first to add a text layer, then convert.

Privacy

Our converter runs entirely in your browser. The file is parsed locally, the spreadsheet is built locally, and the download is generated locally — no upload, no account, no server-side copy. If the table contains client names, salaries, or account numbers, this is the version to use.
